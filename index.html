Life cycle assessments are well established for individual buildings, but their application at neighbourhood scale remains limited.
Infrastructure and planning interactions also influence environmental performance, yet they are often overlooked.
The Quarter Life Cycle Assessment (Q-LCA) model addresses this gap with a modular approach.
It evaluates the Global Warming Potential (GWP) of entire residential neighbourhoods.

The Building Blocks of the Neighbourhood

Every neighbourhood begins with its buildings, including building components, construction methods, and density.
However, buildings are only one part of the neighbourhood system.
It's structure is also shaped by urban elements such as streets and parking areas and underground parking
Another layer is formed by interconnected energy infrastructure.
This includes electricity, utility lines, space heating, and warm water.
Finally, open spaces such as green spaces and unbuilt areas complete the neighbourhood system.
Together, these interconnected elements are assessed over a 50-year life cycle using the Q-LCA model, revealing their contribution to global warming potential.

From Building Blocks to Scenarios

To make the impact of different planning decisions visible, the
Q-LCA model breaks down urban complexity into building blocks.
This allows global warming potential to be evaluated at neighbourhood scale.
The model starts with a standardised one-hectare unit as a basic building block.
These units can be combined to create a complete 6-hectare neighbourhood and explore different planning scenarios.
Each unit is defined by six key factors: Building type, Density, Road layout, Construction method, Heat supply and Energy efficiency level.
Each factor represents a specific planning decision.
For example, Building type describes the configuration of residential units and the overall building volume.
The factor Building type includes six possible typologies: block, line, point, row, duplex and single.
When combined with the factor Density, these choices already create 18 different settlement structures.
High, medium and low densities are the three possible values.
Adding Road layout introduces another layer of variation by including different ways of organising the street network, resulting in 54 possible combinations.
This factor distinguishes between grid, curve and cul-de-sac.
When the Construction method factor is added, with mineral and timber construction as possible values, the number of variations increases to 108.
Adding the Heat supply factor with the three possible values natural gas, local energy and heat pumps results in 324 variations.
The final factor, Energy efficiency level, with values of 15, 30 and 70 kWh, expands the model to 972 possible neighbourhood variations, each of which can be evaluated based on its global warming potential.

How Climate Impact is measured

The GWP of all 972 variations can be explored through different indicators, including total GWP as well as area-based and population-based metrics.
Each indicator highlights different aspects of settlement performance.
Comparing different configurations of urban patterns is therefore not only a matter of total global warming potential but also of spatial efficiency and per-capita resource use.
Changing selected urban development factors reveals shifts in environmental impact, including GWP hotspots and the contribution of different cost groups.
In the Q-LCA model, GWP is structured along the following cost groups:
Building (CG 300)
Building services (CG 400)
Infrastructure (CG 500)
Operational energy (B6)
*Including household electricity

The Model in Practice

The Q-LCA model connects neighbourhood design choices with life cycle impacts.
To see how the model works in practice, we explore one case in detail.

Urban Form & Private Parking

This case compares two neighbourhoods with contrasting urban densities and typologies.
All other factors - road layout, construction method, heat supply and energy efficiency level - remain constant.
In addition, the case examines the influence of private parking infrastructure on global warming potential.
The scenarios are represented at neighbourhood scale, using two six-hectare settlements.
Neighbourhood A represents a compact, high-density urban structure where land is used efficiently and urban functions are closely connected.
Most parking is provided underground to accommodate many vehicles within a limited area, requiring more complex construction and additional materials.

Factor Overview

The Building type factor describes the configuration of residential units and the overall building volume.

The Density factor describes the number of buildings and storeys within a defined settlement unit. Together with the Building Type factor, it defines the spatial structure of the urban layout.

The Road layout factor includes three configurations: grid, curved roads, and cul-de-sacs, representing common street network patterns across different urban forms.

The Construction method factor distinguishes between timber and mineral construction, representing two common structural systems.

The Heat supply factor includes natural gas, local energy systems, and heat pumps. It covers the provision of thermal energy for space heating and domestic hot water, including generation, distribution, and transfer.

The Energy efficiency level factor describes the annual heating demand per square metre of usable floor area (kWh/m² usable floor area *a).
